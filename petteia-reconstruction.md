# Reconstructing *Petteia* / *Polis*
### the Greek game — with *Ludus Latrunculorum* as its Roman reception

*A primary-source reconstruction, built up from the ancient evidence rather than down from the modern rulesets (Bell, Schädler, Murray). Working draft **v0.3** — collapsed from two parallel reconstructions to one canonical game.*

> **The playable edition of this reconstruction is named *Pessoi*** (PESS-oy) — after the counters (πεσσοί) the suitors are playing in the game's oldest witness, Homer, *Odyssey* 1.107. The historical terms *petteia*, *polis*, and *latrunculi* are retained throughout the analysis below; *Pessoi* is the name of the game as you play it.

---

## 0. Method, and why one game

No complete rulebook survives. Every reconstruction is a chain of inferences, and the honest work is marking where each link is iron and where it is wire. Every rule below carries a tag:

- **[A] Attested** — stated or unambiguously implied by an ancient source. Not ours to overrule.
- **[I] Inferred** — not stated, but strongly constrained by the sources plus the behaviour of the wider custodial-capture family (Seega, *tafl*, Kharebga). A scholar may dissent, but bears the burden.
- **[D] Design choice** — genuinely underdetermined. We pick to produce a playable game and say so. These are the rules the engine *tests* rather than asserts (§6).

Two standing commitments: **primary sources outrank secondary reconstructions** (where Bell or Schädler assert what the texts do not license, we drop it — §5); and **we import nothing from a cousin game** except as a tiebreaker where the sources are silent.

### Why we reconstruct one game, not two

An earlier draft built *Latrunculi* and *Petteia* as parallel reconstructions. Carried out honestly, they converged: once the unwarranted accretions are stripped, two equal-army custodial-capture games on a grid have nothing left to keep them apart. The historical record cannot sustain a stable difference, and the reception history proves the danger — every attempt to *differentiate* the Roman game (Bell's "Dux," the leaping king of the popular rule-sheets, a checkmate-style win) pulls it toward chess, while every attempt to keep the two distinct without such accretions collapses them back together. Two failure modes, one cause: there is only one game here.

So we anchor in **Petteia / Polis** and treat *Latrunculi* as its **Roman reception** (§4). The reasons are structural, not sentimental. The Greek game is the **drift-resistant** one: its conceptual core is more completely attested (board, pieces, capture, *and* victory are all Greek-fixed), and its defining feature — victory by **immobilisation** — is the one idea that *cannot* slide into chess, because blockade is the opposite of checkmate. The Roman game can be built clean, but only by continuously overriding its own reception; the Greek game is clean by nature.

**On "reception."** We do not claim a documented line of transmission — the evidence for *direct* Greek-to-Roman descent is sparse. We claim only the parsimonious reading: once anachronisms are bracketed out, the Roman material is most economically understood as the same game received into Latin culture and re-dressed, rather than as an independent invention that happens to share every structural feature. Latrunculi is conventionally "believed to be a variant of the earlier Greek game"; Schädler grants that *polis* and *latrunculi* are "largely the same." Reception is held here as the **most economical hypothesis**, flagged as such, not as fact.

## 1. The evidence dossier

### 1A. The Greek game — the canonical witnesses

| Source | Date | What it actually fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **Homer**, *Odyssey* 1.107 | 8th c. BCE | The suitors play *pessoi*. Existence only — no mechanics. |
| **Plato**, *Republic* 487b–c | 4th c. BCE | The weak player is "**shut in and unable to move**" by the skilled. Fixes **immobilisation/blockade as a decisive condition**, not merely material capture. |
| **Plato**, *Phaedrus* 274c–d | 4th c. BCE | *Petteia* among Theuth's Egyptian inventions → a claimed pedigree; groups *petteia* with dice-games as separate things. |
| **Aristotle**, *Politics* 1253a | 4th c. BCE | The stateless man is *azux* — an "**unpaired piece** in *petteia*." Confirms **isolation = vulnerability**, support = strength. |
| **Polybius** (on Scipio Aemilianus) | 2nd c. BCE | Scipio beat enemies "**by cutting them off and blockading them, like a clever *petteia*-player**." **Blockade**, not annihilation, as the model of victory. |
| **Pollux**, *Onomasticon* IX.97–98 | 2nd c. CE | **The keystone.** The board "played with many pieces," set in lines/cells, is called ***polis*** (city); each **piece is a *kyon*** (dog); pieces are **two colours**; the art is to take a piece "by **enclosing it between two**" of the other colour. (Also names "five-lines" as a *separate* game.) |

**Caution on conflation.** The "five lines" (*pente grammai*) game is a **different**, partly race-based game; popular accounts fold it into petteia. Our *petteia* is specifically **Pollux's *polis***. **Setup and piece-count are nowhere stated**; the repeated "~30 pieces" is Murray's conjecture, not a source.

### 1B. The Roman reception — corroborating and illustrative witnesses

The Latin sources are read here not as a *rival* game's evidence but as the receiving tradition's testimony to the same game — and, crucially, as the place where two questions the Greek record leaves open are answered in practice.

| Source | Date | What it adds to the same game |
|---|---|---|
| **Varro**, *De Lingua Latina* X.22 | 1st c. BCE | The board is a **grid of cells** (compared to a declension table). Corroborates Pollux's board. |
| **Ovid**, *Ars Am.* III.357–360; *Tristia* II.477 | c. 1 BCE–8 CE | **Custodial capture** — a piece caught "between twin foes" perishes. Independent Latin confirmation of Pollux's enclosure rule. |
| **Martial**, *Epigrams* XIV.17 | c. 85 CE | Pieces are *latrones* (soldier-pieces); two colours in opposition; capture by being caught. |
| **Laus Pisonis** 190–208 | 1st c. CE | The richest gameplay source either tradition has. Glass soldiers (*vitreo milite*); pieces **distributed cleverly on the open board** (*…tabula… aperta*) → supplies the **placement** opening; pieces **retreat yet capture the pursuer**; a **reserve** re-enters; one "seemingly checked itself checks two"; play that **breaks the line** → supplies the **slide/burst-through** movement. |
| **Seneca** (metaphor, *alligatus*) | c. 65 CE | Read by Schädler as implying **delayed removal / freeing** of a trapped piece. Interpretive, not explicit — and treated here as a *possible Roman accretion*, not canonical (§4, §5). |
| **Isidore**, *Etymologiae* XVIII | 7th c. CE | Late lexicography: the terms *vagus* (wanderer), *ordinarius* (regular), *incitus* (immobilised). The idiom *ad incitas redigere* ("reduced to immobility" = beaten) independently echoes the blockade victory from the Latin side. |

**Archaeology.** Boards survive at 7×7, 7×8, 8×8, 9×9, 9×10; bone/glass/pottery counters, uniform and distinguished only by colour (the Perugia hoard, the British Museum sets); the Vindolanda and Corbridge boards. The **Stanway grave** (Colchester, c. 50 CE) — rectangular (~2:3), possibly un-latticed — is the sole basis for the "Dux" theory and is plausibly a *different* (native British) game; we set it aside (§5).

**Computational check (Crist, Piette, Soemers, Stephenson & Browne, 2024, via Ludii).** Independent support for two of our calls: **slide movement** is playable and source-consistent; the **large rectangular boards** (8×12 Colchester-type) **stall out** under every custodial ruleset — evidence they were a different game, and that the real game lived on the squarer 7×8–9×9 boards.

## 2. What the sources fix vs. leave open

1. **Board is a grid.** [A] Pollux; Varro. Not in dispute.
2. **Capture = custodial enclosure.** [A] The best-attested rule of all — Pollux, confirmed by Ovid and Martial. Bedrock.
3. **Two players, two colours, otherwise-identical pieces.** [A] Pollux ("dogs… two colours"). A single named piece-type, **no hierarchy**.
4. **No special/leader piece.** [I, strong] *No Greek or Roman text mentions a second piece type.* The Dux rests entirely on the Stanway arrangement; we treat **all pieces as equal** (§5).
5. **Victory by immobilisation / blockade.** [A] Plato, Aristotle, Polybius make *cornering and being unable to move* the defining image. **Foregrounded as the primary win.** Material reduction is a **secondary** path [I], and a **material tie-break** decides a stalled game [I/D] — the plain sense of *ad incitas redigere*.
6. **Board size.** [D] Open — no Greek dimension survives, and the Roman archaeology spreads across 7×7, 7×8, 8×8, 9×9, 9×10 with none privileged. **7×7 default** — equally attested, the squarest of the small boards (inside the computationally-supported 7×8–9×9 range, §1B), and deliberately neither the 8×8 chessboard nor a Go-sized grid, so the game reads as a thing of its own. Size remains tunable; a 9×9 *array* is first-player-skewed (§6B), and board size does not change the blockade outcome (§6C).
7. **Opening — placement.** [I] Nowhere stated in Greek. But the receiving tradition's richest source puts play on the **open board** (*Laus Pisonis*), the opposed-rows array is "a chess analogy for which there is no authority" (Wayte, *CR* 1892), and self-play makes placement the fairest, most varied opening. The Roman reception thus *fills a Greek gap*: **open-board placement**, captures suspended during it.
8. **Movement — slide.** [I] Nowhere stated. The reception again supplies it: the *Laus Pisonis*' "breaking the line" reads as ranged movement, and **single-step movement is computationally degenerate** (games never resolve; §6B) while slide gives a living game. **Slide (rook-like).**
9. **No suicide.** [I] Capture is mover-initiated; moving *into* a pre-existing bracket is safe. Universal in the family and required to function.
10. **Multiple simultaneous capture.** [I] One move closing two brackets takes both. (Echoed by "itself checks two.")
11. **Corner capture.** [D] A corner piece taken by bracketing its two approaches. Near-inert in play (§6B); on by default, toggleable.
12. **No freeing in the canonical game.** [I] No Greek source supports a rescue mechanic. Freeing belongs, if anywhere, to the Roman reception (§4) — and even there is weakly attested and demonstrably pacifying (§6B).

## 3. The reconstruction — *PETTEIA / POLIS*

**Players** 2. **Board** open square grid, **7×7 default**, size tunable [D] (§2.6). **Pieces** the "dogs" (*kynes*) — identical, two colours; player-agreed count, **12 per side** a clean default on 7×7 — roughly half the city occupied [D].

**Opening — placement.** [I]
Starting from an empty board, players alternate placing one dog on any vacant cell until all are down. No captures during placement.

**Movement — slide.** [I]
A dog slides orthogonally any number of unobstructed cells (rook-like); it may not pass through or land on an occupied cell. Mobility is generous on purpose: **blockade is the soul of the game** and needs room to manoeuvre. *(Variant: single-step — but see §6B, where it is degenerate.)*

**Capture — enclosure.** [A]
After your move, an enemy dog flanked on two opposite orthogonal sides by your dogs is **taken and removed immediately**. The bracket must be **closed by the move just made**; a dog that moves *into* a pre-existing gap is **safe** (no suicide). One move may close several brackets; all such dogs fall. *(Corner capture on by default; toggleable. [D])*

**No immediate move-undo.** [D]
A dog may not return on the very next turn to the cell it just left (anti-shuffle).

**Victory.** [A primary; I/D secondary]
1. **Blockade (primary).** You win by leaving your opponent **unable to make any legal move** — the *cornering* of Plato, the *azux* of Aristotle, the blockade of Polybius.
2. **Annihilation (secondary).** Reducing the opponent below the two dogs needed ever to capture is also a win.
3. **Material tie-break.** If a turn cap is reached, the player with **more dogs standing** wins; equality alone is a draw — the plain reading of *ad incitas redigere*, and the rule that (in self-play) turns the austere game from two-thirds drawn to fully decisive without distorting fairness (§6B).

*A note on "primary," per §6C.* Blockade is the **attested** victory and the thematic meaning of defeat — but the engine finds it almost never the way strong play actually *ends* (capture decides). "Primary" here marks what the sources foreground and what losing *means*, not the statistically dominant outcome; under competent play the **material tie-break** is the de-facto decider. This is editorial precision, not a retreat: the texts make immobilisation the image of defeat, never the common result.

## 4. *Ludus Latrunculorum* as Roman reception

We read the Roman game as the same game in Latin dress. The hypothesis is economical rather than documented (§0), but it earns its keep three ways.

**It shares the entire canonical skeleton.** Grid (Varro), equal two-colour pieces (Martial), custodial capture (Ovid) — point for point Pollux's game.

**It supplies the two rules the Greek record leaves open.** This is the strongest reason to keep the Roman material close rather than discard it. The Greek sources fix the *concept* (board, pieces, capture, victory) but are silent on **opening** and **movement** — exactly the two [D] gaps. The receiving tradition's richest source, the *Laus Pisonis*, answers both: play on the **open board** → placement; "breaking the line" → slide. The reception is not decoration; it is evidence for the canonical game's two underdetermined rules.

**It corroborates the victory condition.** The Latin idiom *ad incitas redigere* — "reduced to immobility," i.e. beaten (Plautus, Lucilius; the terminology via Isidore) — independently confirms, from the Roman side, that **immobilisation is defeat**, the very thing Plato and Polybius make central.

**Roman-flavour variants** (offered, not canonical): the military vocabulary (*latrones*, *ordinarii*, the *vagus*→*ordinarius* progression); and the **freeing / *incitus*** mechanic — a bracketed piece frozen for a turn and removed next turn unless a flanker is itself trapped. We treat freeing as a **possible Roman accretion only**: it rests on Schädler's reading of a *Senecan metaphor* (not an explicit rule), it has no Greek warrant, and under skilled play it nearly abolishes combat (§6B). It is the most likely single point of genuine Roman *divergence* — and precisely the kind of differentiating accretion that, pushed further, becomes chess. We log it and leave it off by default.

**Honest caveat.** Direct transmission is sparsely attested. A reader who weights embodied texture over parsimony could instead anchor in *Ludus* (for the *Laus Pisonis* and the archaeology) and treat petteia as ancestral background — equally coherent, but it trades drift-resistance for richness and demands permanent vigilance against the chess-pull. We have chosen parsimony and the drift-resistant core.

## 5. Where we break from the standard reconstructions

1. **No Dux / no leader piece** (vs. **Bell**). The leader-piece has no text behind it; it enters through readings of the Stanway arrangement (Parlett floated it, Bell built it). Pollux's undifferentiated "dogs," the silence of every text, and the uniform physical pieces weigh against it; Schädler reads Stanway as plausibly a native British game. **All pieces equal.**
2. **Blockade restored as the primary victory** (vs. **Schädler/Murray**, who centre reduction-to-one). Plato, Aristotle, and Polybius make immobilisation *the* image of defeat; we promote it, with material as secondary and a material tie-break. *(But "primary" is a claim about attestation and theme, not frequency: the engine shows blockade rarely decides a game even under strong play, and board geometry does not fix this — §6C. We foreground it as the source-attested victory and the meaning of defeat, while acknowledging the material tie-break does the practical work.)*
3. **One game, not two — Latrunculi as reception** (vs. the whole tradition of parallel Greek/Roman reconstructions). Stripped of accretions the two cannot be told apart; we name the Greek game canonical and the Roman game its reception (§4).
4. **Slide movement** (vs. Schädler's single-step), on the *Laus Pisonis*' "breaking the line," the 2024 computational results, and our own finding that single-step is degenerate.
5. **Placement, not opposed rows** (vs. Murray's chess-style array), which has "no authority" (Wayte 1892) and plays worse than placement (§6B).
6. **Board honesty.** The big rectangular boards (Colchester 8×12) are treated as **probably a different game**, following the computational stall-out, rather than stretched to fit.

## 6. The tested program

The [D] rules were not argued to a conclusion in prose but **played to one**: a custodial-capture engine with every rule as a flag, a unit-tested capture core (14 tests), an alpha-beta agent that defends and angles for blockade, confidence intervals over self-play, and a playable client. The canonical preset is the §3 game.

### 6B. What the engine found

- **Single-step movement is degenerate** — step games never resolve through play (they run to the cap, at every search depth and however the cap is scored), while slide resolves cleanly. Firmest result; settles movement in favour of **slide**. [→ §3 movement]
- **Placement is fairest; the array is worst.** A 9×9 *array* hands the first player ~80% of decided games and collapses opening variety; **placement** is the fairest and most varied opening. [→ §3 opening]
- **A material tie-break rescues the austere game** — deciding a capped game by dogs-standing takes the blockade game from ~⅔ drawn to **fully decisive while staying fair**. Adopted. [→ §3 victory]
- **Freeing suppresses combat** — under skilled play the *incitus* rule drops captures from ~18/game to ~0.7/game; a careful player won't risk a capture a counter-capture can undo. Why freeing is a *Roman-reception variant only*, off by default. [→ §4]
- **Corner-capture is near-inert** — rarely binds; settled on coherence, not consequence. [→ §3]

Outstanding when v0.3 was written: a depth-3 run over ~100 games per configuration to tighten the mid-range intervals. That run — and a good deal more — has since been carried out; see **§6C**, which also reports a result that bears directly on §3 and §5.2.

The structured-decision rationale and per-rule confidence grades are kept in the companion document, *Design Decisions & Evidential Standing*.

### 6C. The blockade verdict — an open editorial fork

*Added after v0.3. The §6 program was rebuilt for speed (a bitboard engine, parity-verified against the reference on 2,304 games and exact on the evaluation) and extended to three independent agent architectures — a static heuristic, alpha-beta search at depths 2–3 under three evaluations (one written specifically to seek blockade), and an evaluation-free MCTS. This let us put the one question §6B left implicit: does **blockade**, which §3 and D10 foreground as the* primary *victory, actually decide games under competent play?*

**The result is stark, and it is robust.** Across all three architectures — and again once the placement opening is played competently rather than blindly — **blockade decides essentially no games: zero, over several hundred decided games, with upper 95% confidence bounds of only a few percent.** What decides them is capture — annihilation under sharp play, the material tie-break otherwise. The finding meets the agent-invariance bar this project set for itself (trust a result only when ≥2 architectures agree); here three do, including the eval-free architecture best suited to a siege.

**Why — and it is structural, not a weakness of the players.** Blockade and capture pull against each other. To immobilise an enemy stone you must crowd its neighbours, but crowding it custodially tends to *capture* it (or spring a bracket) first; a true blockade requires keeping enemy stones alive *and* trapped, a delicate global plan no shallow search assembles and no rollout stumbles into. And the movement rule compounds it: **slide** movement gives a hemmed-in stone a long-range escape down any open file, so total immobilisation on an open 8×8 is extraordinarily hard. The irony is exact — slide was adopted *because* single-step is degenerate (§6B), yet slide is also what puts the canonical victory out of reach.

**This forces a choice the engine cannot make.** It is set down here as an open editorial fork, not a settled revision:

- **Response A — editorial precision.** Keep blockade as the victory the sources unambiguously attest — Plato's cornering, Polybius' Scipio, Aristotle's *azux* are not in doubt — but stop letting "primary" imply it is *how strong play wins*. State plainly that under competent play the **material tie-break** (already adopted in §3 for decisiveness) is the de-facto decider, with blockade the rarer, more elegant finish and the thematic image of defeat. This costs the headline that most sharply distinguished this reconstruction from Schädler/Murray (§5.2) — but it is the more defensible reading, because what is graded **[A]** is that blockade is *a* decisive condition and *the* image of defeat, never that it is the statistically dominant outcome.

- **Response B — revisit a [D] rule.** Treat the 0% as evidence a design choice mis-serves the attested goal, and change it. The prime suspect is **slide movement (D6)** — but reverting to step reintroduces the degeneracy §6B rejected, trading one pathology for another. The gentler lever is **board size (D4)**: blockade is markedly easier on smaller or edge-dense boards, several of which are archaeologically attested. The cost is honesty about motive — this would tune a [D] rule not toward the better-*playing* game but toward making an [A] *ideal* also the common outcome, inverting the governing rule that playability chooses only *among* what the sources leave open (§0; Design Decisions §0).

The responses are not exclusive — one could adopt A in the prose now and flag D4 as a live experiment. What the engine establishes is only the fact that forces the choice: **as the canonical rules stand, the soul of the game and the way the game is won are not the same thing.**

*Postscript — the D4 lever, tested.* Response B's gentler option (board size) was then put to the engine: a blockade-seeking agent over boards from 5×5 to 9×9 at two densities. The result discourages it. No board at natural (~half-full) density produced *any* blockade; only small, **unattested** boards (5×5, 6×6) crowded to ~70% fill yielded a 3–7% rate, and the smallest *attested* board, 7×7, gave none even when crowded. Board geometry does not buy back blockade within the evidence — which leaves **Response A** as the resolution, and it is adopted in §3 and §5.2 above. (The same experiment did, separately, motivate moving the default board off the 8×8 chessboard to **7×7** — §2.6 — a distinctiveness gain, not a blockade fix. The run also confirmed the instrument is sound: the engine *does* register blockades where the geometry permits them, so the 0% on the canonical board is a real property, not a blind spot.)
